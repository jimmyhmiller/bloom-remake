# 14 / Gap 4: MapReduce Online (HOP), the BOOM group's own Hadoop successor

Tag used in FEATURES.md: **R14/G4**. This report fills a gap in reports R01–R10: none of them cite *MapReduce
Online* or the Hadoop Online Prototype (HOP). The same BOOM team (Condie, Conway, Alvaro, Hellerstein, with
Elmeleegy and Sears from Yahoo!) wrote it in 2009–2011 as the direct follow-up to BOOM Analytics. HOP's
mechanisms are pipelined shuffle, snapshot outputs, job-to-job pipelining, continuous jobs, pipelined fault
tolerance and alert-driven speculation. Each maps onto an existing item: the BOOM-MR data plane (FLAG-100..104),
shuffle by seals (FLAG-122), lineage recovery (FLAG-123), Tide streaming (FLAG-129) and ODD-19.

Rule sketches in this report use the Molly-style Dedalus pseudo-syntax that R10 uses (`@async`, `@next`,
`notin`, lattice aggregates written `max<X>`). They are marked **SKETCH (ours)**. The real surface syntax is
ODD-01's decision.

## 1. Executive summary for implementers

1. **HOP's thesis.** MapReduce does not have to materialize every map and reduce output before a consumer may read
   it in order to be fault tolerant. HOP pushes **sorted, combined spill runs** from mappers to reducers as they are
   produced. It keeps map output on local disk so failed reducers can recover. It treats pipelined map output as
   **tentative** until the mapper commits. The same user-visible Hadoop API runs unmodified. (NSDI §1, §3)
2. **Three new capabilities fall out of pipelining.**
   - *Online aggregation.* Reducers periodically run the reduce function on the data received so far and publish a
     **snapshot** labelled with a **progress** value.
   - *Job-to-job pipelining of snapshots.*
   - *Continuous jobs.* Map and reduce tasks run forever over streams, the reduce is invoked by a time, logical or
     count trigger, and a map-side **ring buffer** is garbage-collected by reducer consumption acks.

   (NSDI §4–5)
3. **Batch benefit.**
   - 17.7–25% shorter completion on WordCount when reducers are under-utilized.
   - 17% *slower* with a single reducer, because the simple adaptive policy could not push work back to the mappers
     fast enough.
   - The adaptive policy holds back spills when the **stall fraction** is ≥ 0.5, or when combining is effective
     (combined/raw × backlog < 1). It then merges and combines the backlog.

   (NSDI §3.1.3, §6; SRC)
4. **Fault tolerance exists in two versions.**
   - **Paper version**: output is tentative until the map commits, and the output of a failed attempt is discarded.
   - **Talk and code version**: spill files have **deterministic boundaries and sequence numbers**. The reducer keeps
     one **cursor per logical map task, shared by all attempts**, and accepts a spill only if its id is the next one
     expected. That makes retries and speculative attempts idempotent.

   Our design must use both, layered (CR-32).
5. **HOP does not estimate accuracy.** It reports progress: the mean of the per-mapper progress scores, with
   mappers not yet heard from counting as 0. Its own experiment shows that scaling by job progress is badly biased
   (relative error up to about 0.7) and a per-group "sample fraction" is much better (about 0.03). Principled
   estimation needs a random block order *and* a correction for the inspection paradox (PANSARE).
6. **How this maps onto our language.**
   - A snapshot is a **nondeterministic `reveal` of a lattice, triggered by a threshold on a progress lattice**
     (Hydro: "snapshots are monotone"; Flo: snapshots break eager execution unless typed nondet).
   - Per-mapper completion is a **seal carrying a digest**.
   - Tentative output is **partials keyed by (mapper, attempt)** that are released by a commit seal.
   - The downstream "keep the latest snapshot" is a **`LexPair<Max<progress>, Snapshot>` lattice** (LANG-131).
   - Map-side ring-buffer GC is **Edelweiss ARM** (ANA-061).
   - Reducer checkpoints are **ABS barrier snapshots** (FLAG-132). They are automatic because all reducer state is
     declared.
7. **What CALM adds to HOP.** HOP believed that "the final result of the reduce step cannot be produced until all
   map tasks have completed". That holds only for non-monotone reducers. Threshold outputs of lattice reducers
   (`count ≥ k`, `max ≥ v`, key present) are **final early**. Monotone reducers give snapshots that are **sound lower
   bounds forming a chain**. Only algebraic and holistic reducers produce true estimates that downstream consumers
   must be able to retract (ANA-036).
8. **Recommended ODD-19 change.** Deliver HOP parity as milestone **M1b**, immediately after the BOOM-MR parity work
   in M1a: pipelined shuffle, online aggregation, snapshot-pipelined chains, HOP-mode continuous jobs and
   alert-based speculation. Tide (M3) then generalizes HOP's snapshots into triggers and accumulation modes. This
   reproduces the BOOM lineage end to end: BOOM Analytics, then HOP, then Tide. (§11)

## 2. What HOP modified: the stock Hadoop 0.19 dataflow

Taken from NSDI §2 and THESIS Ch. 8. These are the facts the pipelined design depends on.

- **Map task**, two phases.
  - The *map phase* reads a split (one HDFS block by default) and applies `map(K1, V1, OutputCollector)`.
  - The `OutputCollector` partitions each record, `partition = hash(key) mod #reducers` (SLIDES), and buffers
    records in memory.
  - When the buffer is full it **spills**: the records are sorted by (partition, key) and written as an *index file*
    (the offset of each partition) plus a *data file* (key size, value size, key bytes, value bytes) (NSDI Fig. 2).
  - The *commit phase* merges all spill files into one data/index pair and registers it with the TaskTracker.
  - The combiner is applied to sorted runs. Hadoop's `min.num.spills.for.combine` defaults to 3 in the HOP code.
- **Reduce task**, three phases.
  - *Shuffle*: HTTP pulls from at most 5 TaskTrackers at a time; HOP's sink allows `mapred.reduce.parallel.copies`,
    default 20.
  - *Sort*: merge the pre-sorted runs.
  - *Reduce*: `reduce(K2, Iterator<V2>, OutputCollector)`.
  - Output goes to a temporary HDFS file and is then **atomically renamed** into place.
  - A reduce cannot fetch a map's output until that map has committed.
- **Progress accounting.**
  - A map's progress is the fraction of its input consumed, in [0,1].
  - Stock Hadoop weights the three reduce phases 1/3 each. **HOP weights the reduce progress 75% shuffle and 25%
    reduce+commit** (NSDI §6.1 footnote 3; `ReduceTask.run`: `float[] weights = {0.75f, 0.25f}`).
  - This matters because LATE and Hadoop's default speculation both rank tasks by progress rate (FLAG-101/102).
    Pipelining changes what reducer progress means.
- **Fault tolerance in stock Hadoop.** A failed task is simply rescheduled, because "a task never exports any data
  other than its final answer" (NSDI §2.4).

## 3. Pipelining within a job (NSDI §3.1; THESIS §10.1.1; SRC)

### 3.1 The naive design, and why it was abandoned
- Each reducer opens a TCP socket to every mapper when the job starts. Each record is sent to its partition's socket
  as soon as it is produced. The reducer buffers what it receives, spills sorted runs, and does a final merge once
  "every map task has completed".
- The THESIS adds that this "couples the execution of map and reduce task executions, forcing us to schedule all
  reduce tasks before any one map task".
- Four problems with it:
  1. There may not be enough slots to schedule every task.
  2. It needs O(maps × reduces) TCP connections.
  3. The map thread blocks on network I/O.
  4. Eager per-record sending **defeats combiners** and **moves sorting to the reducer**. The reducer then has to
     do a full external sort, which is expensive because "the number of map tasks typically far exceeds the number
     of reduces".

### 3.2 Refinements that HOP kept
1. **Push with a fallback to pull.** "If a reduce task has not yet been scheduled, any map tasks that produce records
   for that partition simply write them to disk." Once the reducer is scheduled, it pulls in the Hadoop way.
2. **A cap on push connections.** A reducer can be configured to pipeline from at most *k* mappers at once. It pulls
   from the rest.
3. **A separate thread for the map function.** The map function writes into an in-memory buffer, and a separate
   SpillThread sends. So a pipeline stall never blocks map progress. (THESIS footnote: the code is based on
   Hadoop's `SpillThread`.)
4. **The spill run is the unit of transfer.**
   - Wait until the buffer reaches its threshold, then sort by (partition, key), apply the combiner, and write a
     spill file in the standard format.
   - The **TaskTracker**, not the task, serves the files. Map tasks register spill files with it over RPC, which lets
     HOP reuse Hadoop's serving code (THESIS footnote 3).
   - When reducers keep up, a spill is sent "soon after it has been produced (in which case, the spill file is likely
     still resident in the map machine's kernel buffer cache)".
5. **Periodic merges at the reducer.** During the shuffle, reducers "periodically perform a merge sort on the already
   received map output", which shortens the final merge (NSDI §6.1).

### 3.3 Adaptive flow control and combining under backpressure

**Paper text (NSDI §3.1.3):**

> "When a map task generates a new spill file, it first queries the TaskTracker for the number of unsent spill
> files. If this number grows beyond a certain threshold (two unsent spill files in our experiments), the map task
> does not immediately register the new spill file with the TaskTracker. Instead, the mapper will accumulate
> multiple spill files. Once the queue of unsent spill files falls below the threshold, the map task merges and
> combines the accumulated spill files into a single file, and then resumes registering its output..."
>
> "The map task records the ratio between the input and output data sizes whenever it invokes the combiner
> function. If the combiner is effective at reducing data volumes, the map task accumulates more spill files (and
> applies the combiner function to all of them) before registering that output..."
>
> Footnote 2: "if the combiner reduces data volume by 1/k on average, we wait until k spill files have accumulated
> before registering them with the TaskTracker. A better heuristic would also account for the computational cost of
> applying the combiner function."

**What the code actually does** (`JOutputBuffer.SpillThread.spill()`, v0.2). It differs from the prose because it
uses a *stall fraction*, not an unsent-file count:

```java
float reduction = sortAndSpill();          // = combinedBytes / rawBytes if a combiner exists, else 1.0
if (pipeline) {
  float stall_frac = umbilical.stallFraction(taskid);
  if (!open || (stall_frac < 0.5f &&
                (reduction * (spills.size() - nextPipelineSpill)) >= 1.0f)) {
    pipeline();                            // send backlog; if >1 file, merge (and combine) first
  } else { /* hold off */ }
}
```

- `stallFraction` is computed by the TaskTracker's per-attempt `FileManager.flush()`: the number of destinations that
  answered `RETRY` in the last flush round, divided by the number of destinations (`Manager.java`). A destination
  answers RETRY when its connection is full or an I/O error occurs.
- `pipeline()`:
  - With exactly one unsent spill, it is sent with that spill's progress attached.
  - With more than one, `mergeSpill(next, last)` merges the whole range into one file. The file carries the id
    range `[next..last]` and the current progress.
- **Sentinel.** On `close()`, if the final forced spill turns out empty, the mapper writes an **empty sentinel spill
  with progress 1.0 and `eof=true`**, so that every reducer still receives an end-of-stream marker.
- **Determinism note from the code:** `doSpill()` says "Ensure deterministic spill files by only taking the first
  call to perform a spill". Spill boundaries are set by the buffer-full points (`io.sort.mb`=100,
  `io.sort.spill.percent`=0.8, `io.sort.record.percent`=0.05). Given a deterministic map function and fixed
  configuration, those points are deterministic.
- **Merged ranges are not deterministic.** Which spills get merged depends on the runtime stall fraction, so the
  merge groupings differ between attempts. See §5.3 for why this matters.

**The measured effect of the policy** (NSDI §6):
- When reducers are under-utilized, pipelining wins.
- "when reduce tasks are already the bottleneck, pipelining offers fewer performance benefits, and may even hurt
  performance".
- With a single reducer the job was about 17% slower: "our simple adaptive flow control scheme ... was unable to
  move load back to the map tasks aggressively enough".
- The THESIS adds, for 20 reducers, a small plateau at 75% progress "attributed to extra disk I/Os ... due to
  diminished effectiveness of the combiner in the pipelining case. ... a job that contains a more effective combiner
  may be better executed in blocking mode."

## 4. Pipelining between jobs, and scheduling (NSDI §3.2, §3.4; THESIS §10.1.2, §10.4.1)

- **Mechanism.** The reduce tasks of job j1 can push their output directly to the map tasks of j2, "sidestepping the
  need for expensive fault-tolerant storage in HDFS for what amounts to a temporary file".
- The TR09 describes the interface: "an asynchronous request call interface exported by each reduce task, through
  which map tasks from other jobs can request snapshots and even the final output. The final output is concurrently
  written to HDFS for fault tolerance, unless otherwise specified."
- In the code, `PipelineMapTask` reads `mapred.job.pipeline` (the upstream JobID) and `mapred.job.input.snapshots`.
- **HOP's stated limitation:** "the computation of the reduce function from the previous job and the map function of
  the next job cannot be overlapped: the final result of the reduce step cannot be produced until all map tasks have
  completed". HOP therefore pipelines only snapshots and final outputs. §10.4 shows CALM refining this.
- **Job submission.**
  - The client submits a **list of jobs**; each job depends on the one before it.
  - The client annotates each job with the id of the job it depends on.
  - The JobTracker "co-schedules jobs with their dependencies, giving slot preference to 'upstream' jobs over the
    'downstream' jobs they feed".
  - In the THESIS version, "a new table ... captured inter-job dependencies", and the scheduling rules consult it.
- **The JOL port** (THESIS §10.4.1).
  - Hadoop 0.19.2's `TaskScheduler.assignTasks(TaskTrackerStatus)` becomes a `taskTracker` tuple update. The fixpoint
    then runs.
  - Tuples inserted into `schedule` fire a Java listener that builds `Task` objects. These are returned when the
    fixpoint ends.
  - The whole port is "55 lines of Java glue code" plus the FIFO Overlog rules.
  - The `job` relation gains a **job type**: pipelining/blocking, online aggregation, or continuous.
- **The online and continuous scheduling rules**, verbatim (THESIS Fig. 10.10; the spacing lost in extraction has
  been restored):

  ```
  h1 unscheduledReduceTasks(JobId, a_count<TaskId>) :-
       job(JobId, JType, ...),
       task(JobId, TaskId, TType, Status, ...),
       JType == JobType.ONLINE, TType == TaskType.REDUCE,
       Status.state() != TaskState.RUNNING;
  h2 canScheduleMaps(JobId) :-
       unscheduledReduceTasks(JobId, Count),
       Count == 0;
  h3 canScheduleMaps(JobId) :-
       job(JobId, Type, ...),
       Type != JobType.ONLINE;
  ```

  - `canScheduleMaps` is added to the map-scheduling rule (s4 in THESIS Fig. 9.2).
  - "A separate admission controller rule ensured that the number of reduce tasks for an online aggregation or
    continuous job fit within the current cluster-wide slot capacity."
  - Rationale for online aggregation: "unscheduled partitions (i.e., groups) may contain important data".
  - Rationale for continuous jobs: scheduling the entire pipeline "is a requirement in order to avoid the memory
    pressure in storing the (continuously arriving) data for an unscheduled operator".
  - **Implementation subtlety for us.** h2 tests `Count == 0`, but under GROUP BY semantics an empty group yields
    **no row** (CR-08). If every reduce is running, h1 derives nothing and h2 never fires. Our port must write
    `count<TaskId> default 0 per job(JobId, online)` (LANG-106). A second point: the prose says "when the number of
    non-running **map** tasks is equal to zero", but the rule counts reduce tasks. The rule is authoritative.
- **Future-work insight** (THESIS Ch. 11): "leaving map tasks unscheduled is akin to sampling the input file, whereas
  leaving reduce tasks unscheduled is akin to missing certain output keys ... This favors reducers over mappers, at
  least during early stages of processing."

## 5. Fault tolerance under pipelining (NSDI §3.3; SLIDES; SRC)

### 5.1 Paper design: tentative map output
Quoted from NSDI §3.3:

- "we added bookkeeping to the reduce task to record which map task produced each pipelined spill file".
- "the reducer treats the output of a pipelined map task as 'tentative' until the JobTracker informs the reducer that
  the map task has committed successfully. The reducer can merge together spill files generated by the same
  uncommitted mapper, but will not combine those spill files with the output of other map tasks until it has been
  notified that the map task has committed. Thus, if a map task fails, each reduce task can ignore any tentative
  spill files produced by the failed map attempt."

**Reducer failure.** "map tasks retain their output data on the local disk for the complete job duration", so a new
reduce attempt can be "sent all the input data that was sent to the failed reduce attempt". TR09 adds: the map
tasks "write a complete output file to disk before committing as in normal Hadoop".

**Proposed "checkpoint", not implemented.**
- "as a map task runs, it will periodically notify the JobTracker that it has reached offset x in its input split.
  The JobTracker will notify any connected reducers; map task output that was produced before offset x can then be
  merged by reducers with other map task output as normal. To avoid duplicate results, if the map task fails, the
  new map task attempt resumes reading its input at offset x."
- NSDI adds that this "would also reduce the amount of redundant work done after a map task failure or during
  speculative execution of 'backup' tasks".

### 5.2 Talk design: deterministic spills with sequence numbers (SLIDES, slide 25, verbatim)

> "Simple PFT design: Reduce treats in-progress map output as *tentative*; If map dies then throw away its output;
> If map succeeds then accept its output.
> Revised PFT design: Spill files have deterministic boundaries and are assigned a sequence number;
> **Correctness**: Reduce tasks ensure spill files are idempotent; **Optimization**: Map tasks avoid sending
> redundant spill files."

### 5.3 What the v0.2 code implements: the revised design

`BufferExchangeSink` (the reducer) and `BufferExchangeSource` (the TaskTracker serving a map attempt) implement the
following protocol.

**Receiver state.**
- `cursor: Map<TaskID, Position>`. The key is the **logical map task, not the attempt**. The comment reads "The
  current input position along each input task (max aggregate over attempts)".
- `successful: Set<TaskID>`, the tasks whose `eof` has been received.
- `inputProgress: Map<TaskID, Float>` and `progressSum`.

**FileHandler.receive(header)**, used for batch spill files. `header.ids()` is a spill-id range `[first..last]`:
```
pos = cursor[T] < 0 ? header.ids().first() : cursor[T]
if header.ids().first() == pos:
    reply READY; read data into the input buffer; updateProgress(header); cursor[T] = header.ids().last()+1
else:
    reply IGNORE
reply cursor[T]            // "Indicate the next spill file that I expect."
```

**FileSource.transfer(file)**, on the sender. It sends only when its own copy of the cursor is absent or equals
`first`. It then adopts whatever next position the receiver reports.

**Stream and snapshot variants.**
- The `StreamHandler` sequence check is `pos <= header.sequence()`.
- The `SnapshotHandler` accepts only when `position < header.progress()`. It is "newer progress wins".

**Progress.** `updateProgress` replaces T's progress with the header's progress, keeping `progressSum` up to date.
Once `successful.size() == numInputs`, the sink is complete. Otherwise
`progress = progressSum / numInputs`, where tasks not yet heard from count as 0.

**Failure events are effectively ignored.** In `ReduceTask.MapOutputFetcher`, the handling for
`FAILED/KILLED/OBSOLETE/TIPFAILED` is:

```java
if (!mapTasks.contains(mapTaskId)) { mapTasks.remove(mapTaskId); }   // no-op
```

This is a no-op. So v0.2 relies entirely on the per-TaskID cursor. A retry or a speculative attempt of the same map
task continues from the cursor. Its early spills are IGNOREd and later spills are accepted. **This is correct only
if spill contents are a deterministic function of (split, map function, config).**

**Two hazards we observed in the code.** These are our analysis, not reported by the authors, and were not tested.
1. **Straddling merged ranges.**
   - Merge groupings depend on the stall fraction, which differs between attempts.
   - Example: attempt 1 delivered spills 0–2, so `cursor = 3`. Attempt 2's backlog was merged as `[2..4]`.
   - `first == 3` fails, so `[2..4]` is IGNOREd, and the sender then skips to position 3, which no remaining file
     starts at.
   - Spills 3–4 are therefore never accepted from attempt 2. Nothing else will send them, so the sink never reaches
     `eof` for that task.
   - Our design must choose merge ranges **per destination, starting at the cursor that destination acknowledged**
     (DIST-011).
2. **Streams GC assuming no speculation.** `Manager.flush` deletes a STREAM file as soon as every partition has
   serviced it, with the comment "Assume no speculations for streaming". A speculative or retried consumer could
   not re-read those files.

### 5.4 Speculation and pipelining
- NSDI §8 lists this as open: "pipeline parallelism ... needs to be integrated intelligently with both intra-task
  partition parallelism and speculative redundant execution for 'straggler' handling".
- In v0.2 the **effective semantics** is that the per-task cursor lets *any* attempt's copy of spill *s* satisfy
  spill *s*. A speculative map attempt that runs ahead fills in later spills, and one that runs behind is ignored.
  Speculation and pipelining compose **iff** mappers are deterministic.
- Reduce speculation works as in Hadoop. The duplicate attempt pulls from retained map output, and the final output
  commit is an atomic rename.

### 5.5 Continuous-job fault tolerance (NSDI §5.1.1)
- Mapper history is unbounded, but many reducers need only a suffix, e.g. a 30-second moving average.
- "Map-side spill files are maintained in a ring buffer with unique IDs for spill files over time. When a reducer
  commits an output to HDFS, it informs the JobTracker about the run of map output records it no longer needs,
  identifying the run by spill file IDs and offsets within those files. The JobTracker can then tell mappers to
  garbage collect the appropriate data."
- For reducers with long histories: "reducers checkpoint internal state to HDFS, along with markers for the mapper
  offsets at which the internal state was checkpointed". The authors note the burden this places on the user: "to
  correctly identify the sensitive internal state".
- They compare it with process pairs (FLuX [Shah et al.]): "far more complex and resource-intensive".

## 6. Online aggregation (NSDI §4; DEMO §4.1; SRC)

### 6.1 Snapshot semantics
- **Definition.** "We can support online aggregation by simply applying the reduce function to the data that a
  reduce task has received so far. We call the output of such an intermediate reduce operation a snapshot."
- **Trigger.** The user specifies snapshot points in units of progress, e.g. 25%, 50% and 75%.
  - The code uses `mapred.snapshot.frequency` (default 1.0 = none) and `mapred.snapshot.max.progress` (default 0.9).
  - In `ReduceTask.copy()`, whenever the sink is notified:

    ```
    if progress > threshold && progress < maxSnapshotProgress:
        threshold += snapshotFreq;  reduce(... snapshot ...)
    ```

  - If progress jumps past several points at once, **only one snapshot is taken** and the threshold advances by one
    step.
  - No snapshot is taken at progress ≥ 0.9 by default. The final reduce happens after the sink completes.
- **Including tentative data.** "The user may also specify whether to include data from tentative (unfinished) map
  tasks. This option does not affect the fault tolerance design."
- **Publication.**
  - Each snapshot goes to an HDFS directory whose name includes the progress. The code's file name is
    `"snapshot-" + fmt(progress) + "-" + fmt(partition)`.
  - It is written to a temporary directory and then **atomically renamed**.
  - "An application knows that a given snapshot has been completed when every reduce task has written a file to the
    snapshot directory."
- **Pitfall.** If not every reducer is scheduled, those reducers' snapshots never appear. The user can detect this
  ("no risk of incorrect data"). The prototype avoided it by configuration, with the proposed fix of "waiting to
  execute an online aggregation job until there are enough reduce slots". That became rules h1–h3.
- **When the reduce itself is pipelined** (`mapred.reduce.pipeline`), the snapshot goes to the downstream job
  through `JOutputBuffer.snapshot()` and `umbilical.output()`, instead of to HDFS.

### 6.2 The progress metric (exact)
- Every spill carries the map's progress score at the time of the spill. A reducer's progress is the mean over **all**
  map tasks of the latest progress it has received from each, with maps it has not heard from counting as 0.
- The paper's wording is "if a reducer is connected to 1/n of the total number of map tasks in the job, we divide
  the average progress score by n". This is the same as `progressSum / numInputs` in the code.
- The authors propose improvements they did not build: map selectivity, the output distribution, and combiner
  effectiveness.
- **HOP reports progress, not accuracy.** "Accuracy estimation is a hard problem even for simple SQL queries ... we
  report job progress, not accuracy: we leave it to the user (or their MapReduce code) to correlate progress to a
  formal notion of accuracy."

### 6.3 Multi-job online aggregation (NSDI §4.2)
- A snapshot of j1 is written to HDFS **and** sent to j2's map tasks. j2 then produces its own snapshot, and so on
  down "an arbitrarily long sequence of jobs".
- **Non-monotonicity.** "the output of a reduce function is not 'monotonic': the output of a reduce function on the
  first 50% of the input data may not be obviously related to the output of the reduce function on the first 25%.
  Thus, as new snapshots are produced by j1, j2 must be recomputed from scratch ... this could be optimized for
  reduce functions that are declared to be distributive or algebraic aggregates."
- **Fault tolerance**, verbatim:
  - "Tasks that fail in j1 recover as described in Section 3.3."
  - "If a task in j2 fails, the system simply restarts the failed task. Since subsequent snapshots produced by j1 are
    taken from a superset of the mapper output in j1, the next snapshot received by the restarted reduce task in j2
    will have a higher progress score."
  - "To handle failures in j1, tasks in j2 cache the most recent snapshot received by j1, and replace it when they
    receive a new snapshot with a higher progress metric."
  - "If tasks from both jobs fail, a new task in j2 recovers the most recent snapshot from j1 that was stored in HDFS
    and then wait for snapshots with a higher progress score."
- **In the code**, `JSnapshotBuffer` holds one `Snapshot` per upstream `TaskID` and replaces it only when
  `this.progress < header.progress()`. That is a max-by-progress register per upstream task.
- **A conflict between sources.** TR09 says "Since the snapshots produced by j1 are monotonic". NSDI changed this to
  "taken from a superset of the mapper output". The NSDI wording is the correct one: the *input* grows monotonically,
  the *output* does not in general. See CR-33.

### 6.4 Experiments and numbers

**Top-K with inter-job online aggregation** (NSDI §4.3, Fig. 4; TR09 §4.3):
- Setup: 5.5 GB of Wikipedia text, 128 MB blocks, 60-node EC2 cluster of "high-CPU medium" instances (1.7 GB RAM,
  2 virtual cores). One node ran the JobTracker and the NameNode.
- j1 is a word count whose reducers output their local top-K. j2's maps reverse (word, count) and sort by descending
  count. A single j2 reducer merges and returns the first K.
- Accuracy is measured post hoc as "the time at which the Top-K words in the snapshot are the Top-K words in the
  final result".
- Read from Fig. 4 (±3 s): Top-5 correct at about **30 s**, Top-10 at about **51 s**, Top-20 at about **83 s**. The
  job ended at about **140 s**. Map progress reached 100% at about 105 s.
- The data "was biased toward these Top-K words (e.g., 'the', 'is', etc.), which remained in their correct position
  throughout".

**Top-5 on Gutenberg** (TR09 §4.1.2, Fig. 6): 550 MB, 5 blocks. The correct Top-5 was available "within about 21% of
the total runtime". Online aggregation "does incur a performance penalty, it is relatively modest".

**Estimator comparison** (NSDI §4.3.1, Fig. 5; DEMO §4.1.1; SLIDES 27–29):
- Data: Wikipedia hourly page views, 7 months, more than 2.5 million articles, **320 GB compressed / 1 TB
  uncompressed, 5066 files**. Each file is one hour's click log and gets one map.
- Query: group by (language, hour of day) and sum the page views.
- The map function was modified to also emit, for each record, "the fraction of a given hour that each record
  represents". All records from one map sum to 1 for that hour.
- The two estimators (SLIDE 27):
  - *Job progress:* final ≈ intermediate × 1.0 / (fraction of input received by reducers).
  - *Sample fraction:* final ≈ intermediate × (total # of hours / # hours sampled). This is computed per group.
- Error metric: |estimate − actual| / actual, averaged over groups.
- Results, read from SLIDE 29:
  - Job-progress error starts around 0.26, rises to about **0.7** near 1,900 s, and falls to 0 by about 4,700–5,340 s.
  - Sample-fraction error stays around **0.03** throughout.
- Fig. 5b / SLIDE 28, taken at hour 16:00 "less than 2 minutes into a ~2 hour job": English final about 2.0×10⁹,
  sample fraction about 1.9×10⁹, job progress about 6.2×10⁹.
- The authors' explanation: "the job progress assumes that this fraction is uniform across all hours, when in fact
  we could have received much more of one hour and much less of another".

### 6.5 What later work fixed (for estimator design)
- **OLA97 §4**, running confidence intervals. After *n* records retrieved **in random order**, with running mean
  Ȳₙ:
  - Conservative (Hoeffding, needs a priori bounds a ≤ v ≤ b):
    εₙ = (b − a) · ( (1/(2n)) · ln(2/(1 − p)) )^{1/2}.
  - Large-sample (CLT):
    εₙ = z_p · (Tₙ,₂(v))^{1/2} / n^{1/2}, where Tₙ,₂(v) = (n − 1)⁻¹ Σᵢ (v(Lᵢ) − Ȳₙ)² and z_p is the (p + 1)/2
    normal quantile.
  - The paper warns that the true coverage of large-sample intervals "can be less (sometimes much less) than the
    nominal probability p".
  - The appendix extends these to SUM, COUNT, VARIANCE and STDEV, and to selections and joins; we did not transcribe
    the appendix tables.
- **PANSARE** (Condie is a co-author), the critique of HOP: "The query estimate assumed a uniform sample of the input
  data but made no modifications to enforce this in the Hadoop scheduler. This led to significant error."
  - The fix: logically order all blocks with a random permutation (`GetNext()`). The scheduler must assign blocks in
    that order, although it may delay.
  - For each block, record its status (done, processing or unassigned), its per-group aggregate if done, the
    scheduling time t_sch (a lower bound while unassigned), its locality, and the processing time t_proc (a lower
    bound while processing).
  - Estimate with a Bayesian model of the joint distribution of (xᵢ, t_schᵢ, t_procᵢ). This handles the **inspection
    paradox**: slow blocks tend to carry large aggregates, so the blocks finished early are biased low.
  - Their lesson: global clock skew between mapper and reducer badly skews the estimates.
  - It was implemented on Hyracks, not HOP.
- **EARL**: bootstrap resampling gives error estimates for arbitrary analytic functions (non-parametric) on Hadoop.

## 7. Continuous queries (NSDI §5; THESIS §10.3, §10.5; SLIDES 43–45; SRC)

### 7.1 Mechanism
- **Map side.** Continuous map tasks need no change, since output already flows. HOP adds a **`flush` API**: "allows
  map functions to force their current output to reduce tasks. When a reduce task is unable to accept such data,
  the mapper framework stores it locally and sends it at a later time." Sources can be "MapReduce job, TCP socket,
  log files, etc." (SLIDE 43).
- **Reduce side.** "the user-defined reduce function must be periodically invoked on the map output available at
  that reducer". The period can be based on:
  - wall-clock time;
  - logical time, e.g. a field of the map output;
  - the number of input rows.

  The output can go to HDFS or anywhere else, e.g. email alerts.
- **The code implements only wall-clock tumbling windows.** `ReduceTask.stream()` runs the reduce whenever
  `now > windowTimeStamp + window` (`mapred.reduce.window`, default 1000 ms), then calls `inputCollector.free()`.
  - Each window's input is therefore **discarded after it is reduced**, which gives tumbling processing-time
    windows.
  - Any state that crosses windows must live inside the user's reducer. In the `CQ.java` example it is a `static
    CQState` holding a per-host list of `HostState` entries, with timestamps taken from `System.currentTimeMillis()`
    **at the reducer**.
- SLIDE 43: "Window management done at reducer. Reduce output is an infinite series of windowed results. Window
  boundary based on time, record counts, etc."
- **Scheduling:** "wait for required slot capacity" (SLIDE 43). The number of tasks is fixed and set by the user.
  Elasticity is future work.

### 7.2 The monitoring application and its experiment
- Agents are continuous map tasks that read `/proc`. Aggregators are continuous reduce tasks, and can be arranged
  hierarchically (rack, then datacenter).
- Load metric: a linear combination of CPU utilization, paging and swap.
- At regular intervals, compare **each host's 20 s moving average** with **the 120 s moving average of all *other*
  hosts**. A host that is more than **2σ** above that is an outlier and raises a *tentative alert*. An alert is
  issued after **10 tentative alerts within a time window**.
- Setup: 7 "large" EC2 nodes, word count on 5.5 GB of Wikipedia with 5 maps and 2 reduces. Thrashing was induced
  about 10 s into the job.
- Result (NSDI Fig. 6): swapping jumps at about 12 s, and the alert fires about 1 s later. That is "notably faster
  than the 5-second TaskTracker-JobTracker heartbeat". SLIDE 44 says the windows were 1, 5 and 15 s load averages.

### 7.3 Reflective monitoring feeding the declarative scheduler (THESIS §10.5; not in NSDI)
- **The monitoring job** is scheduled at bootstrap. It runs one map per TaskTracker and a number of reducers sized
  to the cluster, e.g. one per rack.
- Map output records are `<host, timestamp, pid, measure, value>`, keyed by rack id (or host). Machine-level
  statistics use `pid = 0`.
- Measures (Table 10.1): COMP EST, USER CPU, SYS CPU, RSS, VSIZE, WRITE BYTES, READ BYTES, NET OUT, NET IN,
  SWAP OUT, SWAP IN, PAGE OUT and PAGE IN, all taken from `/proc/stat`, `/proc/[pid]/{stat,io}`, `/proc/net/dev` and
  `/proc/vmstat`.
- The reducers push their results to the JOL instance in the JobTracker over a TCP back-channel.
- **Relations** (Table 10.2):
  - `machineStat(Host, Measure, TimeStamp, Value)`
  - `processStat(TaskId, Pid, Measure, TimeStamp, Value)`
  - `jobStat(JobId, TaskType, Measure, StatContainer)`
  - `taskStat(JobId, TaskId, Measure, TaskType, Value)`
  - `alert(TaskId, TimeStamp, Measure, Description, Severity)`
- **Rules**, verbatim from THESIS Figs. 10.11–10.14 (spacing restored):

```
ts1 taskStat(TaskId.getJobID(), TaskId, Measure, Type, TimeStamp, Value) :-
      taskAttempt(TaskId, ..., TaskState.RUNNING, Pid),
      processStat(Host, Pid, Measure, TimeStamp, Value),
      Type := TaskId.isMap() ? TaskType.MAP : TaskType.REDUCE;
ts2 taskStat(JobId, TaskId, COMP_EST, TaskType, TimeStamp, CompEst) :-
      taskAttempt(TaskId, ..., Progress, ProgressRate, TaskState.RUNNING, Pid),
      JobId := TaskId.getJobID(),
      Type := TaskId.isMap() ? TaskType.MAP : TaskType.REDUCE,
      CompEst := ProgressRate == 0 ? infinity : (1f - Progress) / ProgressRate,
      TimeStamp := java.lang.System.currentTimeMillis();
js1 taskStatList(JobId, TaskType, Measure, a_list<Value>) :-
      taskStat(JobId, TaskId, Measure, TaskType, TimeStamp, Value);
js2 jobStat(JobId, TaskType, Measure, Statistics) :-
      taskStatList(JobId, TaskType, Measure, TaskStatList),
      Statistics := new StatContainer(TaskStatList);
a1  alert(TaskId, TimeStamp, Measure, Desc, Severity) :-
      taskStat(JobId, TaskId, Measure, TaskType, TimeStamp, TaskStat),
      jobStat(JobId, TaskType, Measure, JobStat),
      JobStat.outlier(Measure, TaskStat) == true,
      Desc := JobStat.description(Measure, TaskStat),
      Severity := JobStat.severity(Measure, TaskStat),
      TimeStamp := java.lang.System.currentTimeMillis();
s1  mostRecentCriticalAlert(TaskId, Measure, a_min<AlertTime>) :-
      alert(TaskId, AlertTime, Measure, Desc, Severity),
      Severity.contains("critical");
s2  schedule(Tracker, list<TaskId, MapSlots>) :-
      heartbeat(Tracker, TrackerStatus, MapSlots, _),
      MapSlots > 0,
      mostRecentCriticalAlert(TaskId, Measure, AlertTime),
      (java.lang.System.currentTimeMillis() - AlertTime) < 10000,
      taskStat(JobId, TaskId, COMP_EST, TaskType, TimeStamp, TaskStat),
      jobStat(JobId, TaskType, COMP_EST, JobStat),
      TaskStat < JobStat.percentile(0.25),
      task(JobId, TaskId, ..., Splits, ...), TaskId.isMap(),
      taskAttemptCount(TaskId, Count), Count == 1,
      InputSplits.contains(TrackerStatus.getHost());
```

- **Notes on the verbatim rules.**
  - s1 uses `a_min<AlertTime>`, which selects the **oldest** alert, although the relation is named "most recent".
    Combined with the `< 10000` ms freshness test, this means "the oldest critical alert, if it is under 10 s old".
  - In s2, `TaskStat < JobStat.percentile(0.25)` flags tasks whose **estimated remaining time is below** the 25th
    percentile. The prose says "estimated time to completion is very high relative to equivalent tasks".
  - One of the two statements is a typo, and we cannot tell which. Our port uses the prose's intent: COMP_EST above
    the 75th percentile.
  - `Splits` and `InputSplits` are inconsistent variable names in the figure.
- **Evaluation** (THESIS §10.5.5, Fig. 10.15):
  - Setup: 10 GB of random words, 20 maps, a 20-node EC2 cluster. One faulty map runs normally for a minute and then
    sleeps 1 s between calls to `map`.
  - HOP's alert-based policy launched the backup in **half the time** that Hadoop 0.19.2's policy took.
  - The decision also used a "critically low 'user CPU'" and "critically low I/O activity".
  - The monitoring load made the backup itself run slower, but job response time was "slightly less (a few seconds)".
- **Reduce-task speculation** uses a similar rule without the split test (omitted from the thesis).

## 9. Limitations: stated by the authors, and observed by us

**Stated by the authors:**
- No statistical accuracy estimation (§6.2).
- Snapshots of non-monotone reducers force downstream jobs to recompute from scratch (§6.3).
- The number of tasks is fixed for continuous jobs; no elasticity (§7.1).
- The user must identify reducer state for checkpoints (§5.5).
- The simple adaptive flow control is too weak when reducers are the bottleneck (§3.3).
- Scheduling pipelines and DAGs is left open.
- Integrating pipelining with speculation is open.
- Reducer windows are fixed, simple windows. The slides call for a "better interface for window management" and for
  "support for high-level query languages" (SLIDE 34).

**Observed by us** (in the source code or by analysis):
1. `ReduceTask.MapOutputFetcher` handles failure events as a no-op (§5.3).
2. Merged ranges can straddle between attempts (§5.3).
3. Stream GC assumes no speculation (§5.3).
4. Processing-time windows read the reducer's own clock, so results are nondeterministic and not replayable.
5. Punctuation relies on TCP ordering: the `eof` spill is trusted because each connection is ordered. On an
   unordered channel (our SEM-040), a seal needs a **digest**, such as a spill count (§10.2).
6. The snapshot trigger fires at most once per notification even when several thresholds were crossed (§6.1).
7. The job-progress estimator is biased under non-random block order (§6.4), and under the inspection paradox even
   with random order (PANSARE).

### 10.2 The shuffle as an async channel with deterministic identities (SKETCH, ours)

```
// Map side. The host data path (LANG-186) produces sorted, combined spill batches.
// Every record carries (M = logical map task, S = spill seq, I = index within the spill).
shuffle(@R, J, M, S, I, K, V)@async :- spill_rec(@W, J, M, S, I, K, V), owner(J, K, R);
spill_meta(@R, J, M, S, Prog)@async :- spill_done(@W, J, M, S, Prog), reducer(J, R);
map_seal(@R, J, M, NSpills)@async   :- map_committed(@W, J, M, NSpills), reducer(J, R);

// Reduce side. Set semantics over the identity columns makes duplicates from redelivery,
// retries and speculative attempts vanish. This is the "idempotent spill" of SLIDE 25.
in(J, M, S, I, K, V)        :- shuffle(J, M, S, I, K, V);             // persistent
seen_spill(J, M, set<S>)    :- spill_meta(J, M, S, _);
m_progress(J, M, max<P>)    :- spill_meta(J, M, _, P);
mapper_done(J, M)           :- map_seal(J, M, N), seen_spill(J, M, Ss), size(Ss) >= N;   // digest threshold
ready(J)                    :- all_mappers_done(J);   // ∀ over a sealed mapper set: count<M> mapper_done >= numMaps(J)
```

- **Why the digest.** HOP trusts that `eof` arrives last because the TCP connection is ordered. Our channels are
  unordered (SEM-040), so a seal must say *how many* spills to expect. Blazes TR §2: "Punctuations must contain
  metadata describing the contents of the partition that they seal".
- **Exactly-once without the cursor.** Records are identified by `(M, S, I)` and their contents are deterministic,
  so set union already gives exactly-once. HOP's cursor handshake becomes a **pure optimization** that avoids
  resending ("Optimization: Map tasks avoid sending redundant spill files"). This is the CALM reading of SLIDE 25.
- **When the map side combines records before sending:**
  - Per-record identity is lost, and a combined partial is identified by `(M, S_lo..S_hi, K)`.
  - If the reduce aggregate is a **lattice merge**, which is idempotent, overlapping partials are harmless.
  - If it is **non-idempotent**, such as a sum or count monoid, the receiver must accept only partials whose ranges
    are **disjoint** from ranges already received. That is HOP's cursor. The sender must pick merge ranges *per
    destination*, starting at that destination's acknowledged cursor, which avoids the straddle bug of §5.3.
- **Determinism requirement.** Spill boundaries must be a deterministic function of `(split, map function, config)`.
  So:
  - the **flush API** and time-based spilling are forbidden for dedupable channels;
  - a flushed spill carries `nondet` identity (LANG-204), and its channel is exactly-once only through tentative
    segregation (§10.3);
  - map functions written as rules are deterministic by construction (LANG-181);
  - host UDFs must be declared `pure` (LANG-182), or the job falls back to the tentative scheme.

### 10.3 Tentative output as partials keyed by attempt (SKETCH, ours)

This scheme is required when mapper determinism is **not** certified (ANA-037), and it is always safe:

```
part(J, M, A, K, agg<V>)   <= shuffle(J, M, A, S, I, K, V);        // per attempt; merging within one attempt is allowed
committed(J, M, A)         :- map_commit(J, M, A);                 // exactly one A per M, decided by the JobTracker (FLAG-100)
final_in(J, K, M, X)       :- part(J, M, A, K, X), committed(J, M, A);
reduce_state(J, K, agg<X>) <= final_in(J, K, M, X);                // cross-mapper merge only after commit
// Failed attempts are never committed. Their `part` rows are garbage (Edelweiss DR+ once map_failed(J,M,A) is sealed).
```

- A snapshot may read `reduce_state` only (**committed-only** mode). Or it may read `reduce_state ⊔ ⨆_{M
  uncommitted} part(J, M, latest A, …)` (**include-tentative** mode). That is HOP's user option.
- In include-tentative mode the snapshot's class drops from "lower bound" to "estimate" **unless** mappers are
  certified deterministic. A deterministic failed attempt's output is reproduced exactly by its retry, so it was
  never wrong.

### 10.4 The CALM classification: which reducers may emit early or final results (ANA-036, new)

Let the reduce be a function `f` from the multiset of committed map output to the output. Relative to the
completion seal `ready(J)`:

| Class | Definition | Examples from HOP | Before `ready` | After `ready` |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **T: threshold-final** | The output is a monotone threshold (LANG-126) over a lattice-valued state (LANG-124) | "word count ≥ 1000", "max latency ≥ 5 s", "host seen", bool_or | **Emit as FINAL** as soon as it holds, per key. It is never retracted (ANA-028) | Same |
| **L: progressive lower bound** | `f` is a lattice morphism or a monotone function of the input into a lattice | count and sum over non-negative values (WordCount, page-view sums), set of distinct keys, max | Snapshots are **sound bounds**, `snap(p) ⊑ final`, and they form a **chain** in p | FINAL |
| **A: algebraic estimate** | `f` is non-monotone but decomposable into monotone moments | avg, variance, ratio, the scaled-up sum `x/progress` | Snapshots are **estimates**. They get a confidence interval if the block order is randomized (LANG-113). Downstream consumers need retraction (Z-sets, FLAG-130) or recomputation | FINAL |
| **H: holistic snapshot-only** | Not decomposable, or depends on ranks | median, top-K of counts, argmax | Snapshots are estimates without a principled interval. They are labeled `nondet`. Early finalization is possible only through bounds (FLAG-138, P2) | FINAL |
| **W: windowed** | Any of the above, applied per window | moving averages in the monitoring job | Per window, the class above. The window's seal (watermark ≥ end) makes it FINAL | — |

Implementation rules:
1. **HOP's "cannot overlap reduce of j1 with map of j2" becomes, per output:**
   - class T overlaps **fully**: final tuples stream into j2 as they arise;
   - class L streams lower bounds, and j2 may consume them incrementally *if j2 is monotone*;
   - classes A and H stream estimates that j2 must be able to retract.
2. **Threshold-final needs exactly-once inputs** for non-idempotent lattices. A count computed over a bag with
   duplicates overshoots and could fire a false threshold. So class T and class L over counts or sums require the
   identity-dedup of §10.2, or the committed-only mode of §10.3.
3. **Including tentative data** keeps classes T and L only when mappers are certified deterministic (ANA-037).
   Otherwise the output is downgraded to A or H.
4. Every snapshot row carries its class: `snap(J, P, point, class, …)`. The per-output determinism certificate
   (ANA-029) lists snapshot streams as "nondeterministic by design (progressive)", with the class as a refinement.

### 10.5 Snapshots as Flo/Hydro-style progressive outputs (LANG-139, new)

A HOP snapshot is **exactly** Hydro's `singleton.snapshot(&tick, nondet!)` applied to an unbounded monotone
singleton. Hydro guarantees that "Snapshots are monotone". Flo explains why a raw `to_sequence`/`reveal` of a
growing lattice violates *eager execution*: its value depends on scheduling. So the construct must be typed
nondeterministic, unless it is read only through a threshold (class T).

Proposed surface (SKETCH, ours):

```
snapshot wc_snap of word_count          // word_count: lattice-valued, per key
  at progress every 0.1 upto 0.9       // HOP: mapred.snapshot.frequency / max.progress
  mode committed_only                  // or include_tentative
  estimate scale_by coverage(hour);    // optional estimator (§10.6)
```

It desugars to:

```
progress(J, R, mean_over<numMaps(J)>(P)) :- m_progress(J, M, P);                        // unheard mappers count as 0
snap_point(J, R, Pt)                     :- snapshot_points(J, Pt), progress(J, R, P), P >= Pt;   // threshold
snap(J, R, Pt, K, reveal(C)) nondet "HOP progressive snapshot"
                                         :- snap_point(J, R, Pt), notin snap_taken(J, R, Pt), word_count(J, K, C);
snap_taken(J, R, Pt)@next                :- snap_point(J, R, Pt);
snapshot_complete(J, Pt)                 :- count<R> snap_published(J, R, Pt) >= numReducers(J);  // threshold: final
```

- **Semantics.** `snap(Pt)` holds `f` applied to the prefix the reducer had at the **first tick where progress ≥
  Pt**. The prefix is a nondeterministic *subset* of the input.
  - For class L this implies `snap(Pt₁) ⊑ snap(Pt₂) ⊑ final` whenever Pt₁ < Pt₂, **per reducer incarnation**.
  - After a reducer restarts, `snap ⊑ final` still holds, but the chain may restart. §10.9 covers publication
    rules.
- **Deviations from HOP.**
  1. When several points are crossed in one tick, we emit a snapshot for **every** crossed point, all with the same
     value. HOP emits only one.
  2. The published progress value is the **actual** progress at snapshot time. HOP names the directory by the actual
     progress, but its trigger threshold advances by fixed steps.

### 10.6 Progress and accuracy estimators (LANG-113, new)

Three estimators, in increasing quality:
1. **Job progress** (HOP): `est(k) = snap(k) / progress`. It is included for parity and must always be labeled
   biased.
2. **Coverage-scaled, the "sample fraction"** (HOP §4.3.1, generalized):
   - Each record may carry a coverage weight `w` for a stratum `h`, such as the fraction of an hour.
   - The coverage lattice is `cov(h) = sum<w>`, with a known total `W(h)`.
   - `est(k) = snap(k) · W(h(k)) / cov(h(k))`, defined when `cov > 0`.
   - The built-in form is `scale_by coverage(h)`. `cov` is a class-L sum, so it is monotone.
3. **Randomized-order online aggregation** (OLA97; PANSARE):
   - The scheduler draws input blocks from a seeded random permutation (FLAG-135). Under simulation the seed is the
     run seed.
   - For N blocks, with n done and per-block group aggregates xᵢ, the estimate is `T̂ = N · x̄ₙ`.
   - The large-sample interval is `T̂ ± z_p · N · (Tₙ,₂)^{1/2} / n^{1/2}`. A finite-population correction
     √(1 − n/N) is optional; that factor is standard survey sampling and is **not** in OLA97.
   - The conservative interval uses Hoeffding with declared bounds.
   - Every interval must carry the caveat that it assumes processing time is independent of block contents. If a
     `timing_correction` flag is set, the per-block (t_sch, t_proc, status) statistics are recorded so that a
     PANSARE-style estimator can be plugged in later (P2).

### 10.7 Job-to-job snapshot pipelining (FLAG-136, new; SKETCH, ours)

```
// j2 map side. Keep only the newest snapshot from each upstream j1 partition U.
latest(J2, U, lexpair<max<P>, Snap>) <= snap_in(J2, U, P, Snap);        // LANG-131: the chain key is progress
j2_input(J2, U, K, V)                :- latest(J2, U, (P, Snap)), member(Snap, K, V);
```

- **Failure cases, all subsumed by the lattice:**
  - a restarted j2 task simply re-receives, and a stale snapshot is dominated;
  - a failed j1 reducer resends from progress 0, and those snapshots are dominated until they pass the old progress;
  - if both fail, the latest snapshot is recovered from the durable snapshot store and then dominated as before.

  **The final snapshot (P = 1.0, eof) dominates everything, so j2's final output is deterministic** (confluent given
  j1's seal).
- **Computing j2 when a new snapshot arrives** (HOP recomputed "from scratch"):
  - If j1 is **class L and j2 is monotone**: j2 consumes the delta `Snap_new \ Snap_old` incrementally (semi-naive).
  - If j1 is class A or H, or j2 is non-monotone: feed j2 the **Z-set difference** `Snap_new − Snap_old`, with
    weights +1/−1, through the incremental view machinery (FLAG-130, DBSP chain rule).
  - This is exactly HOP's suggested optimization "for reduce functions that are declared to be distributive or
    algebraic".
  - Recompute from scratch is the fallback only for opaque host reducers.

### 10.8 Adaptive send policy and combiner derivation (DIST-012, LANG-112, new)

- **Combiner derivation** from the reducer's aggregate. Legal is a property of the aggregate; effective is measured
  at runtime.
  - A lattice merge gives the combiner `merge`.
  - A commutative monoid (sum, count) gives a partial fold.
  - Algebraic aggregates (avg, var) give moment tuples `(n, Σx, Σx²)`.
  - Holistic aggregates get no combiner.
  - A user-defined aggregate (LANG-105) gets a combiner if it is declared associative and commutative.
- **The send policy**, parameterized as in HOP and evaluated per spill:
  `send ⇔ closing ∨ (stall < σ ∧ reduction × backlog ≥ 1)`. HOP's values are σ = 0.5 and reduction = combined/raw.
  When sending a backlog of more than one spill, merge and combine it **per destination, from that destination's
  acknowledged cursor**.
- **CALM-aware extensions** (ours):
  1. Class T or L reducers downstream favour early sends, because they yield early final results or bounds.
     Class A/H reducers with an effective combiner favour batching.
  2. The single-reducer case that HOP lost by 17% is handled by a **reverse-pressure rule**: when all consumers'
     stall ≥ σ for k consecutive rounds, switch the channel to blocking mode with a full map-side sort and combine
     (Hadoop's layout), until stall < σ/2.
- **What must be tested.** The final output is identical under every policy, because the policy changes only
  batching. BENCH-191 does this.

### 10.9 Recovery and speculation

- **Recovery through lineage (FLAG-123).**
  - `derived_from(reduce partition R, map task M, spill S)` is exactly HOP's bookkeeping, "record which map task
    produced each pipelined spill file". It is coarse-grained provenance.
  - When a reducer is lost, a new attempt re-requests every sealed spill from the retained map outputs.
  - When a retained map output is lost as well, the map task is recomputed from its split, which is deterministic,
    and the same `(M, S, I)` identities are regenerated.
  - Retention is reclaimed when **all** reducers of the job have committed. That is DR+ over the per-reducer commit
    seals.
- **Prefix seals (FLAG-109), from HOP's proposed "checkpoint".**
  - A mapper periodically announces `(M, offset x ↦ spill s)`. That seals spills below s for M, independent of the
    attempt.
  - Reducers may merge those spills across mappers even in the tentative scheme, and a new attempt resumes reading
    at x.
  - This is sound only if the map is **per-record stateless and deterministic** (ANA-037). Map-side combining that
    crosses the offset must restart at a spill boundary.
- **Speculation (FLAG-110):**
  - Map attempts share the logical identity. Whichever attempt delivers spill s first satisfies it, and the
    receiver's set ignores the rest. This is the v0.2 behaviour, made safe by the determinism certificate. Without
    the certificate, use §10.3, where only the committed attempt counts.
  - Reduce attempts commit output with first-wins put-if-absent keyed by the partition (FLAG-124).
  - **Snapshots from speculative reduce attempts:** publish with put-if-absent on `(J, R, point)`. The contents can
    differ between attempts because snapshots are nondet, so the published snapshot records its attempt id.
  - Pipelining changes reducer progress semantics (75/25 weights). LATE's estimated time left for reducers must use
    the *pipelined* progress definition, so FLAG-102 has to be parameterized by the progress model.
- **Alert-based speculation (FLAG-112).** The monitoring job is a continuous job in our system. Its outputs are the
  monitoring relations, and rules s1/s2 become a speculation policy module next to LATE. `currentTimeMillis()`
  becomes `now()` (LANG-171), and the freshness window becomes a timer-based soft state (SEM-060).

### 10.10 Continuous jobs become Tide (FLAG-137, new)

- **HOP-compatibility triggers.**
  - `every <dur> processing-time`, tumbling, discarding.
  - `every <n> rows`.
  - `on logical field <f>` = event time.

  Processing-time triggers are **nondet** (LANG-204). The only deterministic triggers are logical ones:
  event-time windows closed by watermark seals (FLAG-129).
- **State between windows** lives in declared lattices and relations, not in `static` host objects. So checkpoints
  are automatic: an ABS barrier (FLAG-132) captures the reducer state together with each upstream's cursor. This is
  HOP's "markers for the mapper offsets", with no user burden.
- **Map-side ring buffer.** The retention of the outbox is governed by consumer acks: when a reducer commits a window,
  it acks the spill-id ranges it no longer needs. This is ARM (ANA-061) with a range-compressed ack set. Unlike HOP,
  GC waits until **all** consumers, including speculative ones, have acked.
- **Monitoring query in declarative form** (SKETCH, ours; event time is the sample timestamp `T`):

```
load(H, T, L)                  :- sample(@H, T, Cpu, Page, Swap), L = wc*Cpu + wp*Page + ws*Swap;
host_avg(H, W, avg<L>)         :- load(H, T, L), window(T, 20s, W);            // sliding 20 s, per host
others(H, W, mean_sd<L>)       :- load(H2, T, L), H2 != H, host(H), window(T, 120s, W);
tentative(H, W)                :- host_avg(H, W, A), others(H, W, (M, S)), A > M + 2*S;
alert(H, W)                    :- count<W2> tentative(H, W2), within(W2, W, span) >= 10;   // threshold
```

## 11. How HOP changes ODD-19 (the Tide / BOOM-2 milestones)

R10 B4's delivery plan is M1 (FS2 + batch with seals and lineage, reproducing BOOM Analytics), M2 (Lakehouse), M3
(Tide streaming) and M4 (progress tracking). HOP sits **between M1 and M3**. It is the BOOM group's *own* next step
after BOOM Analytics, and it needs only machinery that M1 already builds: seals, lattices, the scheduler as rules,
and host data paths. Recommended amendment:

- **Split M1.**
  - **M1a, "BOOM parity"** (unchanged): FS2, BOOM-MR scheduler (FCFS, LATE), blocking MapReduce, fqpath, and Raft
    for metadata.
  - **M1b, "HOP parity"** (new), with its tests in BENCH-186..192:
    - pipelined shuffle with deterministic spills and idempotent receive (FLAG-105..107);
    - tentative output and prefix seals (FLAG-108/109);
    - speculation under pipelining (FLAG-110);
    - scheduling rules h1–h3 and job chains (FLAG-111);
    - online-aggregation snapshots with classes and estimators (FLAG-135);
    - snapshot pipelining between jobs (FLAG-136);
    - HOP-mode continuous jobs with processing-time triggers and ring-buffer GC (FLAG-137);
    - reflective monitoring with alert-based speculation (FLAG-112).
- **Make pipelining the default in M1b.** Shuffle by seals (FLAG-122) is specified as *pipelined* shuffle by seals,
  and blocking becomes the degenerate send policy. The adaptive policy (DIST-012) and the CALM class decide per
  channel (ODD-24).
- **M3 generalizes M1b instead of replacing it.**
  - A HOP snapshot is a Tide trigger ("fire at progress ≥ p") in accumulating mode.
  - HOP's "downstream recomputes from scratch" becomes accumulating-and-retracting mode (Z-sets).
  - HOP's processing-time windows become event-time windows with watermark seals. Processing time is kept as a
    labeled nondet compatibility mode.
  - HOP's reducer checkpoints become ABS.
  - HOP's ring-buffer GC becomes the durable input log's retention by consumer frontier (FLAG-131).
- **Move part of M4 earlier.** The per-reducer progress lattice from M1b is the simplest instance of M4's frontier
  machinery: a progress score is a scalar frontier. The M4 verification target (the frontier never runs ahead) should
  first be proven for the M1b snapshot trigger, "a published snapshot's progress ≤ the true committed fraction",
  which is small enough for LDFI and bounded model checking.
- **Showcase value.** M1b is the cheapest milestone that exhibits **all three CALM output classes in one system**:
  final-early thresholds, lower-bound snapshots, and retractable estimates. It also reproduces a second BOOM-lineage
  paper end to end.

## 13. MUST-IMPLEMENT CHECKLIST

1. **Push-based spill channel.**
   - Map output moves as sorted, combined spill batches over a partitioned async channel.
   - The sender keeps the output until the consumer commits.
   - The consumer pulls when it was not scheduled when the output was produced.
   - Push connections per consumer are bounded, with pull used for the rest.
   - Source: NSDI §3.1.2–3.1.3.
2. **The map function runs in a separate thread from the sender.** A network stall never blocks map progress.
   — NSDI §3.1.2
3. **Deterministic spill boundaries and ids.** Spill cut points are a function of (split, map function, config),
   and each spill has the id `(task, seq)`. Time-based flushes are marked nondet. — SLIDES 25; SRC `doSpill`
4. **Idempotent receive keyed by the logical task.** Set semantics over `(task, seq, idx)`, or, for combined
   partials, disjoint ranges from a per-destination cursor. Duplicates from retries and speculation are ignored.
   — SLIDES 25; SRC `BufferExchangeSink`
5. **Cursor handshake as an optimization.**
   - The receiver replies with the next expected id and the sender skips.
   - Merge ranges are chosen per destination, starting at the acknowledged cursor, which avoids the straddle bug.
   - Source: SRC `FileSource`; §5.3.
6. **Sealing with a digest.** Per-mapper completion is a seal carrying the spill count, and the reducer's
   `mapper_done` is a threshold on the received set. It does not depend on ordered delivery. — Blazes TR §2; §10.2
7. **Empty sentinel.** When a mapper closes with an empty buffer, it still sends an eof spill with progress 1.0.
   — SRC `SpillThread.close`
8. **Adaptive send policy.** `send ⇔ closing ∨ (stall < σ ∧ reduction×backlog ≥ 1)`. The stall fraction is the
   share of destinations that returned RETRY in the last round, and the reduction is combined/raw. The backlog is
   merged and combined before it is sent. — NSDI §3.1.3; SRC `JOutputBuffer`, `Manager`
9. **Derived combiner.** The map-side partial aggregate is derived from the reducer aggregate's declared properties
   (merge, monoid, moments). Holistic aggregates get none. — NSDI §3.1.3; LANG-105
10. **Reverse-pressure rule.** Switch to blocking layout when every consumer is stalled for k rounds. This fixes
    HOP's single-reducer loss. — NSDI §6.2 (Fig. 9); ours
11. **Tentative partials.** Map output is kept per (mapper, attempt). Merging across mappers only after the commit
    seal. Failed attempts are ignored and reclaimed. — NSDI §3.3
12. **Include-tentative switch for snapshots**, with class downgrade unless the mappers are certified deterministic.
    — NSDI §4.1; §10.4
13. **Prefix seals from map checkpoints.**
    - The mapper announces (offset x ↦ spill s).
    - Reducers merge the prefix across mappers.
    - A retry resumes at x.
    - Requires the map to be certified stateless and deterministic.
    - Source: NSDI §3.3 (proposed).
14. **Map output retained until all reducers commit.**
    - A reducer retry re-fetches everything.
    - If retained output is lost, the map is recomputed from lineage.
    - Source: NSDI §3.3; FLAG-123.
15. **Speculation under pipelining.** Attempt-agnostic spill identity for deterministic maps. First-wins commit for
    reducers and for snapshots, recording the attempt id. — SRC; NSDI §8
16. **Pipelined progress model.** Reduce progress is 0.75 × shuffle + 0.25 × (reduce+commit). The shuffle progress
    is the mean over all maps of the latest per-map progress, with unheard maps counting as 0. LATE and default
    speculation use this model when a job is pipelined. — NSDI §6.1; SRC `ReduceTask`, `BufferExchangeSink`
17. **Progressive snapshot construct.**
    - Points `every Δ upto max` (defaults 1.0 = off, max 0.9), or an explicit list.
    - One snapshot per crossed point.
    - Carries the actual progress value.
    - Typed nondet (progressive), with a class label.
    - Source: NSDI §4.1; SRC.
18. **Snapshot publication.** Atomic per (job, reducer, point). A completeness threshold fires when every reducer
    has published a point. The published snapshot is durable, for recovery. — NSDI §4.1
19. **Early-emission classes T, L, A, H and W** on every reducer output. They decide final-early emission, bound
    semantics, and whether a downstream consumer needs retraction. — §10.4; ANA-028
20. **Estimators.**
    - Job-progress scale-up, labeled biased.
    - Coverage-scaled per stratum (sample fraction).
    - Randomized-order CLT and Hoeffding intervals for sum, count and avg.
    - Source: NSDI §4.3.1; OLA97 §4.
21. **Randomized block order.** A seeded permutation of input blocks that the scheduler must follow, which makes
    online-aggregation intervals meaningful. Per-block timing statistics are recorded. — PANSARE §2
22. **Snapshot pipelining between jobs.**
    - `LexPair<Max<progress>, Snapshot>` per upstream partition.
    - Monotone downstreams consume deltas.
    - Others get Z-set differences, and recomputation is used only for opaque reducers.
    - Source: NSDI §4.2; SRC `JSnapshotBuffer`.
23. **Job-chain submission.** A dependency table, co-scheduling, and slot preference for upstream jobs. — NSDI §3.4;
    THESIS §10.4.1
24. **Rules h1–h3 plus admission control.** For online and continuous jobs, all reduces are running before any map
    is scheduled. Uses `count … default 0` (LANG-106). — THESIS Fig. 10.10
25. **Continuous jobs.**
    - Unbounded map sources, and a flush operation.
    - Reduce triggers by processing time, row count or logical field.
    - Tumbling discard as HOP's default.
    - Deterministic event-time windows as our default.
    - Source: NSDI §5.1; SRC `ReduceTask.stream`.
26. **Ring-buffer retention governed by acks.** The consumer acks spill-id ranges after it commits, and the sender
    reclaims once all consumers, including speculative ones, have acked. — NSDI §5.1.1; ANA-061
27. **Automatic reducer checkpoints with upstream cursors**, as barrier snapshots of the declared state. — NSDI
    §5.1.1; FLAG-132
28. **Monitoring job.**
    - One continuous map per worker reads OS counters.
    - Reducers per rack.
    - Relations `machineStat`, `processStat`, `taskStat`, `jobStat` and `alert`.
    - Source: THESIS §10.5.1–10.5.2.
29. **Outlier and alert rules**, with statistics containers (mean, stddev, percentiles) as incremental aggregates.
    — THESIS Figs. 10.11–10.13
30. **Alert-based speculation.** The oldest critical alert under 10 s old, plus high estimated completion, plus a
    free slot, plus a local split, plus exactly one attempt so far: launch a backup. — THESIS Fig. 10.14
31. **Monitoring as a windowed query.** The 20 s host average against the 120 s average of the other hosts, 2σ
    outliers, and 10 tentative alerts per window. — NSDI §5.3
32. **Bound-based early finalization (P2).** An interval knowledge lattice per key lets top-K and similar outputs
    finalize before `ready`. — ours

## 14. TEST PROGRAMS

Every test runs under the deterministic simulator (TEST-001). Each has a fixed seed and virtual time, so expected
values are exact, reproducible golden results. Where a statistical claim is made, the pass criterion is stated over
a fixed set of seeds.

**T1. Online-aggregation WordCount whose estimates converge** (BENCH-186).
- *Input:*
  - 64 blocks of 10,000 words each, drawn from a Zipf(s = 1.1) vocabulary of 5,000 words with a per-block seed. Total
    640,000 words.
  - 64 maps and 8 reducers.
  - Snapshots every 0.1 up to 0.9.
  - Randomized block order (20 fixed order seeds).
- *Expected:*
  1. The final output equals the single-node reference exactly.
  2. Every snapshot count satisfies `snap_p(w) ≤ final(w)`.
  3. Within one reducer incarnation, `snap_p(w)` never decreases as p increases.
  4. At every p, the job-progress estimate of the **grand total** is within 0.5% of 640,000. Blocks are
     equal-sized, so progress is proportional to the words seen.
  5. For the 100 most frequent words, the mean relative error of `snap/progress`, averaged over the 20 seeds, is
     non-increasing in p. It is 0 at the final output.
  6. Empirical coverage of the 95% CLT interval (block-level samples) over 20 seeds × 100 words × 9 points is at
     least 0.90.
  7. Kill map attempt m7 after its 2nd spill. Results 1–3 still hold in both committed-only and include-tentative
     modes, and no count is inflated.
  8. Negative control: turn off identity dedup. The simulator must produce a run in which some `final(w) >
     reference(w)`, which shows the test is sensitive.
  9. Snapshot classes: `count` snapshots are labeled L, a `count(w) ≥ 1000` output is T, and an `avg` word length
     output is A.

**T2. Sample fraction versus job progress** (BENCH-186, part b; reproduces NSDI §4.3.1 exactly).
- *Input:*
  - 24 hours × 4 blocks per hour, processed in file order: hour-major, not random.
  - Every block of hour h has the *identical* per-language page-view totals, and hour volumes follow a diurnal curve.
  - Map records carry fraction-of-hour weights, with each block contributing 0.25 to its hour.
- *Expected:*
  1. For each group (language, h), the sample-fraction estimate equals the final value **exactly** whenever
     coverage(h) > 0.
  2. At progress 0.25, the job-progress estimate is exactly 4× the final value for groups whose hour is fully seen,
     and 0 for groups whose hour is unseen.
  3. The mean relative error of job progress is ≥ 0.5 at p = 0.25, and the sample-fraction error is 0 over covered
     groups.

**T3. Pipelined two-job chain with a mapper killed mid-stream** (BENCH-187).
- *Program:* j1 is WordCount whose reducers emit a local top-100. j2's maps reverse (word, count), and a single j2
  reducer merges the sorted lists and returns the top-100, with ties broken by word ascending. This is TR09 §4.3.
  - Input: the 640k-word T1 corpus.
  - Snapshot pipelining is on, with snapshots every 0.1.
- *Fault schedules:*
  - (a) kill a j1 map attempt after its 2nd of 5 spills;
  - (b) (a) plus a speculative duplicate of the same map task that runs ahead;
  - (c) kill a j2 map task after it has consumed the 0.3 snapshot;
  - (d) kill a j1 reducer after it has published 0.5;
  - (e) (c) and (d) together (the "both fail" case).
- *Expected:*
  1. In every schedule, j2's final output equals the batch pipeline, meaning j1 blocking and then j2 blocking over
     HDFS files, exactly: the same 100 (word, count) rows in the same order.
  2. The sum of j1's final counts is 640,000.
  3. For every upstream partition, the progress values j2 applies are strictly increasing.
  4. In (c), the first snapshot applied after the restart has progress ≥ 0.3.
  5. In (e), j2 recovers the 0.5 snapshot from the durable store before any newer one.
  6. Negative control: with dedup off in (b), a final count differs from the reference.
- *LDFI:* Molly-2 over the shuffle and snapshot protocol, with one crash and message omissions up to the bound,
  finds no counterexample to "final = batch" when dedup is on, and does find one when dedup is off.

**T4. Continuous windowed job: the HOP monitoring scenario** (BENCH-188).
- *Setup:*
  - 7 simulated hosts, each emitting a load sample every 100 ms of virtual time.
  - Baseline load is 10 ± 1 (seeded). Host h3 steps to 60 at t = 10 s.
  - The query is §10.10's: 20 s per-host average against the 120 s mean and standard deviation of the other hosts,
    2σ, and 10 tentative alerts. The trigger period is 100 ms.
- *Expected:*
  1. Exactly one alert, for h3, and none for any other host.
  2. The alert time equals the time computed by the single-node reference evaluator on the same event-time trace, to
     the tick. With event-time triggers the output is deterministic.
  3. With HOP-compatible processing-time triggers instead, the output is labeled nondet, and only the set of alerted
     hosts {h3} is compared.
  4. A variant is a windowed WordCount over a stream: tumbling 10 s and sliding 30 s/10 s event-time windows.
     - Kill a mapper mid-window and restart it from its retained ring buffer.
     - Every window's counts must equal the batch counts over the same events.
     - After reducer commits, map-side retention is at most the unacknowledged range (at most 2 windows' worth of
       spills per mapper at steady state).
     - No spill is reclaimed before every consumer, including a speculative reducer, has acked it.

**T5. Pipelining versus blocking performance** (BENCH-189; ratios only, reproducing NSDI §6).
- *Setup:*
  - WordCount over 10 GB of uniform random words, 512 MB splits (20 maps), 10 workers with 2 map + 2 reduce slots.
  - Reducer counts R ∈ {1, 5, 20}.
  - Also 100 GB with 240 maps / 60 reduces (512 MB), and with 3120 maps (32 MB), on 20 workers with 4 map + 3 reduce
    slots.
- *Expected:*
  - Pipelined time ≤ 0.85 × blocking for R = 5 and R = 20 (HOP: 0.823 and 0.803).
  - Adaptive time ≤ 1.05 × blocking for R = 1 (HOP: about 1.17; the reverse-pressure rule must fix this).
  - Pipelined ≤ 0.80 × blocking at 100 GB with large blocks (HOP: 0.75).
  - Every configuration gives byte-identical output.

**T6. Adaptive send-policy unit tests** (BENCH-191).
- *Scripted:*
  - (a) no combiner and no stalls: every spill is sent on its own, so the number of pipelined files equals the number
    of spills;
  - (b) a combiner with reduction 0.25: spills are sent in merged groups of 4;
  - (c) stall = 0.6: nothing is sent until stall < 0.5, and then the backlog is sent as one merged file per
    destination, starting at that destination's cursor;
  - (d) close with an empty buffer: exactly one eof sentinel spill with progress 1.0;
  - (e) the straddle scenario of §5.3 (a retry whose merge groupings differ): no hang, and no loss or duplication.
- *Expected:* in all cases the reducer output is identical to blocking mode.

**T7. Online and continuous scheduling rules** (BENCH-192).
- *Setup:* 6 reduce slots cluster-wide.
  - An ONLINE job with 8 reduces is held by admission control.
  - An ONLINE job with 6 reduces has zero maps scheduled until all 6 reduces are RUNNING. The `default 0` count
    must make h2 fire.
  - A batch job submitted at the same time schedules maps immediately.
  - A two-job chain gives the upstream job's tasks slot preference.
- *Expected:* the exact schedule traces from the scripted heartbeats match the golden file.

**T8. Alert-based speculation** (BENCH-190; reproduces THESIS §10.5.5).
- *Setup:* WordCount, 20 maps, 20 simulated workers. Map m5 runs normally for 60 s of virtual time and then sleeps
  1 s between records. Its simulated CPU and I/O counters drop.
- *Expected:*
  - The alert-based policy launches exactly one backup for m5 (`Count == 1`).
  - It does so in ≤ 0.5 × the virtual time Hadoop's default policy (FLAG-101) takes on the same trace.
  - No backup is launched for any other task.
  - The output is exact.

**T9. Bound-based finalization** (P2, under BENCH-186).
- *Setup:* in T1, each reducer tracks `lo(w) = snap(w)` and `hi(w) = snap(w) + remaining_words(R)`.
- *Expected:*
  - Every top-10 membership fact that the reducer declares final before `ready` is in the reference top-10.
  - At least the top-3 words are declared final before progress 0.9 on the Zipf corpus.
